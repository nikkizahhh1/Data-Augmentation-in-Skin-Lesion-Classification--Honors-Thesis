# **Research Problem**

Skin cancer can be one of the easiest-to-miss forms of cancer, simply because it is so perfectly hidden in plain sight. That simple spot on the back of your arm that you may have just thought was just due to tanning could very well be cancerous, and you’d never know. Being able to detect skin abnormalities that blend in so easily is crucial in preventing the growth of these skin cancers. A detection algorithm that has shown promise in detecting skin lesions of all varieties is machine learning, which performs well in dermoscopic classification (Esteva et al. 2017; Nguyen et al., 2022).

A common barrier that exists, however, is the lack of labeled data surrounding skin lesions. More specifically, it has been a barrier in training machine learning models to identify the correct skin lesions. Because in order for these models to be trained properly enough to achieve a certain level of accuracy, a large range of varying data is needed (LeCun et al., 1998). However, not only is data limited to very few skin lesions, but the broader data sets are typically hard to access. Datasets such as the HAM10000 revolutionized the area, bringing a variety of skin lesions, from benign to cancerous to precancerous.

However, even such datasets cannot fully address the issue of limited data (Nguyen et al., 2022; Khan et al., 2024). Within the HAM10000 itself, there is imbalanced data amongst different lesions; for instance, more labeled images of a specific lesion compared to another. The presence of such an imbalance can cause other lesions (that are equally as common) to be easily missed (He & Garcia, 2009; Johnson & Khoshgoftaar, 2019). The detection of a lesion with 100 represented images versus a lesion with 1000 images varies vastly in identification and precision. Thus, this raises the question: can we achieve high performance in a machine learning model without the expensive process of data collection, or is there a way to make up for this gap?

Data augmentation is a technique that has been proposed as a solution. Datasets can be augmented through methods such as distortion, rotation, cropping, flipping, and even noise, and through this process, they can broaden the range of data the model is exposed to and sometimes even improve performance (Shorten & Khoshgoftaar, 2019). Although it is case dependent, a smaller data set expanded through data augmentation can show performance levels similar to a larger dataset within that same domain. However, it is unclear whether data augmentation has a clear promise in skin lesion classification. Factors such as subtle visual differences between benign and malignant lesions can be difficult to detect due to the sheer precision in detail. This study aims to investigate whether data augmentation can help maintain classification performance on the HAM10000 dataset as the amount of data used for training is reduced progressively, observing to what extent it is a substitute for a full dataset.

We also assume that the data the model trains on and the data the model is tested on are both fair representations of the overall dataset. We assume that the test set remains unseen as the model is being trained on the training set. We further assume that any data augmentation or class balancing is applied only to the training set, not the test set, in order to ensure unbiased evaluation of model performance.

We assume all images are of good quality, with proper lighting and resolution, so the model does not have added barriers to image recognition under difficult conditions. This allows us to better hold all external variables constant, so we can truly evaluate the performance of the model with augmented data under controlled conditions.

Finally, we assume our dataset is representative of the real-world lesions prevalent day to day (Tschandl et al. 2018). More specifically, we assume our dataset captures a real-world representation of all the different lesions dermatologists are exposed to in their day-to-day practice. This is essential in our study as ultimately, we aim to improve this model's performance in practical, clinical settings.

# **Theoretical Issues**

In order to assist in the identification of skin lesions accurately and precisely, we can enlist the help of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) that can read and classify images accordingly. CNNs take in images by evaluating each pixel as an input, calculating loss, and backpropagating accordingly until the desired output is reached.  Through each layer of the CNN, more precise detection happens; first starting with just edges and colors, and then on to more complex patterns and shapes. CNNs are specifically specialized in image reading and visual data. CNNs are the standard algorithm for image recognition as they learn spatial patterns and can capture different features within an image, i.e., various textures in a lesion (Krizhevsky et al, 2012).

Delving deeper, CNNs are even more essential in our research as they have been shown in prior studies to achieve strong performance in skin lesion classification. One reason is due to the hierarchical feature extraction of CNNs, using multiple convolutional and pooling layers to detect distinct high and low-level features (LeCun et al., 1998). For instance, they automatically detect low-level features such as edges and color gradients, and have had success in detecting more complex features such as asymmetry or border irregularities. CNNs have performed very well in identifying key diagnostic features regardless of outlying image factors such as position, orientation, or scale. Accuracy rates on the HAM10000 dataset range between 93% to 98%, many times outperforming the accuracy of human dermatologists (Brinker et al., 2019).

Our chosen dataset, the HAM10000, is a dataset that aims to broaden the types of skin lesions represented in datasets. They include images from a wide variety of skin lesions, in an effort to diversify the datasets on which convolutional neural networks are trained to detect skin lesions. The HAM10000 dataset contains a total of roughly 10,000 images, with 7 distinct classes of skin lesion categories, and is a widely used benchmark dataset in skin lesion classification research. However, there is still a data imbalance observed, with more data available for certain lesions in comparison to others. Models strongly prefer a majority class to a minority class, so when presented with two classes of different quantities, they will more than likely favor the more abundant one (He & Garcia, 2009). This is because the majority of classes produce lower errors, and with a model prioritizing better performance, it will train further with the majority class.

Models tend to ignore minority classes during training, as a method of optimizing for correctness (Johnson & Khoshgoftaar, 2019). This bias towards a majority class distorts their learning, despite the fact that they are producing high accuracy rates. For instance, the majority classes typically skew the overall gradient calculated, as they are the more represented class and have a higher abundance of gradients available. Eventually, this leads the network to learn the majority class better since there is a high training signal coming from that class. The combined accuracy rate produced is also misleading, because although the accuracy of the majority class is high, the minority class accuracy will subsequently be low. Despite the fact that the combination of their accuracies for the overall network may be very high, it is not accurate and thus misleading. In short, the majority classes get higher training signals from the various examples available, making the model stronger, while the minority classes get weaker learning signals and more monolithic data, thus leading to worse performance in those classes (He & Garcia, 2009).

A common approach to equalize the size of two different classes is to augment a portion of the minority class's data and add that augmented data to the class, increasing the class size. The idea is that through augmentation, the core category of the data is preserved, only slightly altering it enough to create varied data that may assist in training variability. Data transformation may include methods such as rotation, zoom in, or added noise, while still preserving the true component of the image (Perez & Wang, 2017). Ideally, this increased dataset will allow for sufficient training and learning of the network, as explained previously. There is now a wider range to perform gradients, gradient descent, and backpropagation precisely, improving accuracy in each step (Shorten & Khoshgoftaar, 2019).

When evaluating a network trained on an unbalanced dataset, it is important to note that a generalized accuracy level will not capture the true performance of the model. Thus, it is crucial to utilize evaluation metrics that strongly integrate a minority class just as thoroughly as the majority class. An example can be the F1 metric, which calculates accuracy by leveraging precision and recall. Such a method effectively measures a model's accuracy in identifying skin lesions on an imbalanced dataset, such as the HAM10000 (Sokolova & Lapalme, 2009).

# **Literature Review**

The use of machine learning in dermatology has expanded significantly over the last decade, especially in the area of skin lesion classification. Researchers have found that deep learning models are capable of identifying visual patterns in a dermoscopic image that are often difficult for the human eye to detect. As a result, many studies have explored the use of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to classify skin lesions and assist in the early detection of skin cancer. These models have demonstrated promising results, showing that machine learning can serve as a valuable tool in supporting clinical decision making (Esteva et al., 2017; Brinker et al., 2019).

One of the most influential developments in the area was the introduction of the HAM10000 dataset by Tschandl et al. The dataset was created to address the lack of publicly available dermatology datasets that contained a broad range of pigmented skin lesions. Consisting of over 10,000 annotated dermoscopic images across seven diagnostic categories, the HAM10000 quickly became one of the most widely used benchmarks for skin lesion classification research. The dataset represented a major step forward in creating more diverse and accessible data for machine learning in dermatology. However, despite its size and variety, the dataset still exhibits substantial class imbalance, with certain lesion types being represented more heavily than others (Tschandl et al., 2018).

Because of this imbalance, researchers have increasingly focused on methods that improve performance on minority classes. One approach has been the use of data augmentation techniques, which increase the size and variability of a minority class for training data. Traditional augmentation methods include image rotation, flipping, cropping, scaling, and the addition of noise. These techniques aim to expose a model to a broader range of examples while preserving the core characteristics of the lesion being classified. Existing studies have shown that augmentation can improve model generalization and reduce overfitting, especially when the data available is limited (Shorten & Khoshgoftaar, 2019).

More recent work has explored advanced augmentation methods through the use of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs, Goodfellow et al., 2014). Rather than transforming existing images, GANs generate entirely new synthetic images that resemble real lesions. Studies have shown that these synthetic samples can improve classification performance for underrepresented classes by increasing the amount of available training data. Researchers have suggested that GAN-based augmentation may be especially useful in medical imaging, where collecting and labeling large datasets is costly and time-consuming (Khan et al., 2024).

Researchers have also investigated improvements to classification architectures themselves. For example, Nguyen et al. proposed a skin lesion classification framework that combines a convolutional backbone network with Soft Attention and patient metadata. Their work addressed two challenges commonly found in dermatology datasets: class imbalance and the difficulty of identifying subtle visual differences between lesion types. By incorporating both image information and supplementary patient data, the model demonstrated improved classification performance compared to traditional image-only approaches (Nguyen et al. 2022).

Although these studies all demonstrate that deep learning can improve skin lesion classification, they differ in the approaches they take to overcoming limited data. Esteva et al. (2017) focused primarily on demonstrating the capabilities of deep convolutional neural networks using large datasets, while Nguyen et al. (2022) improved classification performance through architectural enhancements such as Soft Attention and the inclusion of patient metadata. In contrast, Shorten and Khoshgoftaar (2019) and Perez and Wang (2017) emphasized improving the quality and diversity of training data through augmentation techniques rather than modifying the network itself. Similarly, Khan et al. (2024) explored synthetic image generation to increase the representation of minority classes. Collectively, these studies suggest that both model architecture and dataset quality contribute to classification performance. However, relatively few studies isolate the sheer effects of traditional data augmentation alone on class imbalance within the HAM10000 dataset. This leaves an opportunity to evaluate just how much performance improvement can be achieved without introducing more complex network designs.

Although prior research has shown that both CNN-based classification and data augmentation can improve skin lesion recognition, there is still uncertainty regarding the extent to which augmentation can compensate for limited or imbalanced training data. Existing studies often focus on maximizing classification accuracy through increasingly complex architectures, while fewer studies focus on whether augmentation alone can reduce the performance gap between majority and minority lesion classes. This study builds upon existing literature by examining the role of augmentation within the HAM10000 dataset and evaluating whether increasing the representation of minority classes can improve the model.

# **Research question**

The HAM10000 dataset is a widely used dataset that encompasses a wide range of various types of skin lesions. However, the imbalance in representation of certain lesions versus others is apparent; for instance, the Nevus class includes about 6,000 images, whereas the Dermatofibroma (DF) includes roughly 150 images (Tschandl et al., 2018). The size of the minority classes may not be enough to accurately train a network well enough that it will generalize to unseen data in various testing environments. However, where feasible to expand that dataset using augmentation techniques, it warrants further investigation if the results are notably better than our baseline, a model trained on the imbalanced HAM10000 data.

We aim to investigate whether data augmentation of minority classes within the HAM10000 dataset (such as Dermatofibroma or Vascular lesions) will improve the model performance compared to our baseline model trained on the original imbalanced dataset. We measure whether augmentation reduces the performance gap between a minority class and a majority class, such as Nevus.

# **General Research Plan**

Study Overview:

We are doing a computational experiment on skin lesion classification using the HAM10000 dataset. We begin with a baseline: a convolutional neural network trained on the full HAM10000 dataset. We then identify classes within the dataset that are minorities, and apply augmentation techniques to increase their representation within the dataset. Using metrics such as F1 or recall allows us to precisely measure whether the augmented data differs in performance compared to our baseline model. The convolutional neural network serves as our extraction and classification model, learning the seven distinct hierarchies of lesions and classifying them accordingly. Data augmentation is expected to improve performance by increasing variability in minority class samples (Perez & Wang, 2017). The baseline and experimental models follow exactly identical procedures, only differing in class distribution and training data. This way, performance can be evaluated on the overall and class-specific metrics to understand what differentiates minority class behaviors.

Data Source \+ what data is:

The HAM10000 is our primary data source in this experiment, a database of 10,015 annotated images of various skin lesions. This dataset contains 7 distinct categories of skin lesions, such as melanocytic nevi, melanoma, benign keratosis-like lesions, basal cell carcinoma, actinic keratoses, vascular lesions, and dermatofibroma (Tschandl et al., 2018). These are all the major diagnostic categories for pigmented lesions, including malignant and benign classes. This dataset also includes data from various hospital departments, under various lighting conditions and equipment setups, of different ages, genders, and anatomical locations on the body. A label is attached to each image, pertaining to the type of skin lesion observed. Likewise, its metadata contains image ID, lesion ID, how the diagnosis was verified, age, sex, and localization (Tschandl et al., 2018). Verification of the diagnosis is achieved through histopathological exams (biopsy), follow-up, consensus, or confocal.

Experimental Design:

We begin by establishing our baseline model, which is our control group. We split our entire data set into our training set and our testing set, the training set being the bulk of the data, and a small unseen portion being set aside as the test set to assess the performance of the model. We train a convolutional CNN using the HAM10000 dataset, reproducing prior literature (Nguyen et al., 2022). Despite the data imbalance in the HAM10000, we will train our baseline on the full dataset, with the train/test split. This entails observing the performance after we split our data into a training set and a test set, and assessing the performance of the model by observing its performance on the test set. This baseline reflects current practices in skin lesion classification and provides a benchmark for all subsequent models to be evaluated against.

This model serves as our focal point of comparison; any changes made to our experiment moving forward will be held in comparison to the F1 metric we achieve from our first baseline. It serves as our control group, being trained on our unaltered, unaugmented dataset. When future datasets have augmented data added to balance the dataset, and thus the model is trained on a balanced dataset with augmented data, we can measure the success of its performance by comparing it to our first baseline. Any improvement or decline in performance can therefore be attributed to the experimental changes rather than differences in the model architecture or experimental procedures. Our first baseline will measure the impact of augmentation alone, allowing us to observe whether data augmentation improves model performance or not. Keeping our architecture, hyperparameters, and evaluation metrics constant is key to ensuring a fair and accurate result. This isolates augmentation as the primary independent variable throughout the experiment.

We then consider a second baseline model, where we balance the data by removing from the majority classes. We balance the HAM10000 dataset, keeping all classes of equal size, as that is essential to guarantee fairness when training our model. We reduce the majority class’s size until we have equality with the minority classes. This baseline model is equally important, as it allows us to understand whether the size of training data impacts the performance of a model. Comparing this baseline to the first baseline allows us to distinguish whether changes in performance are due to class balance itself or simply due to the amount of available training data. We observe the performance after using the train/test split.

Our independent variable is augmented data. We balance the minority classes with the majority classes by augmenting a portion of data from said minority class and adding that augmented data to the dataset (Khan et al., 2024). Now, our model will be trained on a balanced dataset. The architecture will remain the same across all conditions, and performance will be compared to understand the effect of augmentation. By comparing this model to both baseline models, we can evaluate whether augmentation is capable of improving minority class performance while maintaining the advantages of a larger training dataset. We split our data using the same train/test split, and assess its performance using metrics such as F1.

Throughout it all, all models use the same architecture and are evaluated on the same test set, being evaluated by the same metrics consistently. This consistency ensures that any differences we observe in performance can be attributed to changes in the training data rather than external experimental factors.

Data processing / reduction:

Images from our dataset used as inputs will be preprocessed through resizing, normalization, and division into train, validation, and test sets. Four experimental data augmentation is applied to minority classes. A percent of each minority class will have augmentation techniques applied such as image rotation, zoom, or even added noise (Shorten & Khoshgoftaar, 2019). This is put together with the rest of the minority class to create a new minority class with increased size, balancing its size with the rest of the classes. Outputs of the model will be evaluated for accuracy using metrics such as F1, recall, and classified for further valuation with confusion matrices.

Evaluation metrics:

When trying to understand how effective a model is, the common approach is to focus on the model's accuracy to assess its performance. Accuracy is the percentage of predictions the model answers correctly out of all predictions made, the correct predictions divided by the total number of predictions. However, in an imbalanced dataset, the majority classes will skew this metric, overruling the minority classes. Thus, that accuracy metric does not capture the performance of the model as a whole, leaving room for exclusion of poor performance in many smaller classes. Thus, additional metrics must be utilized that give weight to every aspect of a model's performance equally.

A suitable metric is the F1 score, a metric that gives a harmonic mean of precision and recall combined. Precision measures how many positive predictions from the model are truly correct, measuring the reliability of the model's positive predictions. Using precision, we can evaluate how often the positive results are actually correct in our model. Recall measures the percentage of actual positive cases among all positives identified, true or false. The F1 score combines precision and recall using a harmonic mean to more effectively balance precision and recall. Thus, both values need to be high for the F1 score to be high, and low performance in any aspect of the model will affect the total F1 score. In the case of minority classes, F1 prevents the model from ignoring underrepresented classes (Sokolova & Lapalme, 2009).

We chose F1 over accuracy, precision, and recall due to the nature of this metric being able to evaluate exactness and completeness without allowing a majority class to inflate or skew the results. Accuracy measures overall correctness, so a model can achieve high accuracy on a dataset, even when the majority of the positive predictions are false positives. Precision alone is also not ideal due to its prioritization of quality over quantity. Meaning, if a model only identifies one true positive prediction and makes no false positive predictions, it will give 100% precision, despite the many true positive cases it has missed. Recall is also not ideal here, as it measures the ratio of true positives to all actual positive observations. However, when recall is considered on its own, a model may achieve a high recall while also producing many false positives. Thus, a high recall is not necessarily representative of our model’s overall performance.

Data quality / validity:

We ensure reliability by maintaining clear work conditions. By keeping the model architecture the same across all conditions, we eliminate any unforeseen variables that may skew the data. We use the same division of data across all training and testing to ensure all models are evaluated under identical conditions. Likewise, augmentation was only applied to the training set to ensure the experimental variable is isolated and tested properly.

# **Anticipated Difficulties and Pitfalls**

The most prominent setback we have is class imbalance, where some classes, such as Vascular Lesions (VASC) and Dermatofibroma (DF), account for less than 3% of the entire dataset combined. We combat this by utilizing data augmentation; however, even then, there are still risks. For instance, overfitting augmented images may be a difficulty we may encounter. This is when our model may memorize the distorted version of an image, rather than recognizing a holistic and generalizable pattern to improve its learning of the lesion. This problem typically arises when augmentation is done improperly in an unrealistic manner. For instance, a zoom that is so aggressive you cannot really see much of the image to derive that it's a skin lesion. Signs we watch out for to notice overfitting of augmented data can be when accuracy is high but test accuracy is significantly lower, or when training loss consistently decreases over time while validation loss begins to go back up. However, there are still methods to combat this issue, should it arise. Softening your augmentation techniques is one of the biggest ones, as reducing the intensity of your transformations can preserve the core components of the original image. Likewise, another prevention mechanism is mixing augmented data with raw data, which is exactly what we are doing (Shorten & Khoshgoftaar, 2019).

We may encounter some difficulty in one of our earliest stages, creating our baseline model. Since we are reproducing an existing CNN, we must follow the architecture and methods as precisely as possible, as the subtlest difference may have a significant impact on performance. For instance, if data is processed in the exact method as the published model, it can create performance gaps. Exact resizing methods, pixel normalization sequences, or channel order are all details not typically included in published papers, but are meaningful details that can impact the model's performance. Likewise, there is an issue of hidden hyperparameters due to many papers only highlighting learning rates, but not batch size, weight decay, or other meaningful details that can impact gradient descent. We can prevent such a setback by making sure the model we choose to reproduce is very transparent and thorough in documenting their model configuration and training process. Papers where the authors provide their code publicly are also helpful. Performing a quick test run against a small, well-known dataset to make sure the network performs correctly can also be an approach to find any bugs before the real training begins (Pineau et al., 2021).

The most unwanted setback that may arise is that the augmentation does not have an impact on model performance. It is possible that augmentation will have the opposite effect we want it to, and even confuse the model. If augmentation is used in conjunction with a class that already has sufficient natural variance, augmentation may have little to no benefit. The network may be able to handle natural variations itself and not need artificial variance. Likewise, it can even hinder the learning; for instance, flipping an image may change the appearance of the original image entirely, making it appear to be something completely different, and thus the model will interpret it as such. This is essentially hindering its learning, confusing the model. Thus, it is essential that augmentations are done precisely and are relevant. Each image transformed must not hide the true meaning of the image; for instance, the brightness should only be turned up to the point where you can still tell it is melanoma, not to the point where it now looks like a papule (Khan et al., 2024; IEEE Paper, 2024).

# **Anticipated Benefits**

Class imbalance is a real problem in machine learning as it biases the model towards the majority class, and thus leading the model to fail on the edge cases that may not be as apparent but still exist. It also significantly discredits traditional performance metrics such as accuracy, causing us to resort to a variety of other metrics to monitor performance and learning. Thus, it is important for researchers to understand augmentation benefits, as it can aid in cases of imbalance and stretch limited datasets in a cost-effective manner. It can help researchers avoid overfitting when dealing with smaller datasets, so the model can be exposed to a wider, varied dataset and be able to adapt and generalize properly. Likewise, augmentation allows researchers to prepare models for real-world testing, such as camera angles, varied lighting, or, in our case, various skin tones. It allows the model to learn from a broader dataset that improves its pattern recognition, thus performing better on new or unseen data. The accessibility that the success of data augmentation brings can extend beyond the scope of dermatology; it can be applied in various contexts, and if conducted correctly, may have significant strides in numerous respective fields (Johnson & Khoshgoftaar, 2019).

Within our scope itself, data augmentation can have tremendous strides in research if successful. It will allow for extension of datasets through a very cost-feasible method, and this is crucial as a lab may be underfunded or just too small. It extends accessibility of skin lesion testing to a larger audience, removing many barriers such as limited datasets being scarce, and sufficient datasets being nearly inaccessible or absurdly costly. A well-performing CNN that is exposed to a large variety of skin lesions and has demonstrated success can be a great aid in the clinic setting as a whole. While many screen tools currently exist, such a model can aid in the diagnosis of skin lesions, identifying them more quickly. In a way, if trained sufficiently, it has the potential to outperform existing tools, due to the success CNNs have had in pattern detection within other domains (Brinker et al., 2019).

It is important to note that although our dataset consists of real patient skin lesions, our augmented images are not real patient data. And while the augmented data can improve model performance, it is possible that improvements in the dataset may not transfer to real-world clinic settings well. Poorly designed augmented images can introduce risks and create misleading artifacts, leading the model to fail when interacting with real patients. This is due to the model being trained on artificial patterns and thus learning these artificial patterns that it will seek when exposed to unseen data (Goodfellow et al., 2014).

The goal of this model is not to replace physicians or claim that augmented images are 100% always beneficial. Rather, we aim to understand where augmentation stands within our problem as a whole. We aim to see if augmentation can potentially bring us closer to successfully closing gaps in imbalance datasets using methods accessible and feasible to a wider audience. Yet we are also seeking to understand if our predictions are false as a whole, and to understand why augmentation failed in propelling us to our goal, in the case that it did.
